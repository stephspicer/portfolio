Natural design system adoption, by following the company's tides

Design system evolution and adoption can be hard, but I'm proud of what I achieved here, rarely by asking permission but largely by understanding other priorities and team needs, and attaching design system growth to it. Here's three years of design systems work at Upgrade, a fintech that kept shipping.

Design Systems In-house

Overview

Timeline

2022 — present

My Role

Principal Product Designer, Design Systems Co-owner

Team

Visual design, product design, front-end engineering

Tools

Figma, Storybook, Jira, Figma Code Connect

The design team had been lean and moved fast when I joined Upgrade in 2022. They just migrated from Sketch, so the Figma components were really sparse, colors failed a11y checks, and there wasn't much was documented yet.

In the beginning, I was able to quickly & successfully build and get adoption for dozens of "new" components by building it alongside a 0-1 new product.

I was designing Upgrade's new B2B product, home improvement loan portals. To ship the MVP, we wanted to reuse a lot of design and code from the card and loan products.

I went looking for reusable pieces from the existing products, then organized, architected, sought feedback and published components as I needed them.

Because I was making components we had in products but didn't have in the system yet, adoption from other designers was natural.

Token foundation

But we didn't have a coherent foundation or basic alignment with the codebase: like semantic color tokens, consistent spacing, a color system that passed accessibility, typography styles. Lucky for us, the company had also committed to a brand refresh that year. So I jumped on it.

I later learned Dan Mall (who I have learned so much from!) would refer to this method of design systems implementation as a Trojan Horse. I worked with the visual design team on color, typography, and iconography, and presented to product and engineering to align on the token structure. And because it was attached to a business priority, I was able to get it shipped - yay!

Engineering partnership

Getting the token work into production required close collaboration with engineering from the start

Identified an engineering partner early
Presented proposed changes to the full engineering team
Documented every change thoroughly before implementation
Worked with developers and QA throughout the rollout

Adoption

Because the update was tied to real visual changes designers needed to make anyway, adoption of the token system wasn't a hard sell either.

And once we had synced tokens and spacing that matched what devs would actually use, designs became more trustworthy guidelines. Eng started pushing back when something didn't align, because now alignment was achievable. That kind of feedback loop hadn't really existed before.

When we later acquired another company and needed to onboard their product into our design system, the process went pretty smoothly and quickly because we had just about everything needed in a reusable way to rebuild their product too.

Growing pains

Then the team grew. We started at 4 product designers, today we have 15. When we were small, everyone roughly knew what everyone else was working on. Then weekly crits split into subteam meetings and full team time got less frequent. Designers would end up building the same UI independently, or styling similar things differently without realizing it and new UI wasn't extracted into the system to be used.

I experimented with design systems office hours, and a slack channel, and that went okay, but by end of 2025 we started to outgrow that too.

Research

I interviewed all 15 product designers to understand where the system was and wasn't working for them

Governance

The goal was to make the design system feel like a resource, not a rulebook. We formalized ownership, built a decision framework for when to reuse versus diverge, and replaced our existing office hours with a UI Show and Tell where designers share new UI patterns with the team. The contribution process became clearer and documentation more consistent. The point was to give everyone somewhere to go and something to trust.

UI Show and Tell

The UI Show and Tell gave designers a specific thing to bring

Net new UI created to solve a unique problem — not full flows, not work made entirely from existing components.

Results

Three years in, the system feels like what we were trying to build

Designers are reusing components across products, having cross-product UI conversations that wouldn't have happened before, and surfacing component gaps as a team rather than solving them independently. The design system is used by a team of 50+ front end engineers, and we're currently working with them on Figma Code Connect to tighten the loop between design and code even further.

Design systems work rarely shows up on a roadmap. Getting this much built, adopted, and continuously improved at a company moving this fast is what I'm most proud of in my time here.
